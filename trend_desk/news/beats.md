# News Desk: what's different about judging notices and releases

The News Judge reads this after Trend Desk's `beats.md` (who Meagan is and what each destination
wants). Edit it freely; standing rules from her go in `trend_desk/rules.md` (shared) or below under
"Her rules for news".

## What the items are

- **press_release**: a company's or firm's own announcement (Business Wire, PR Newswire). Every
  claim in it is made by someone with a stake in it. Never state it as fact.
- **org_release**: an advocacy group's or trade body's announcement. Also an interested party.
- **agency**: a federal agency's release (CMS, FDA, NIH, CDC, CFPB...). A primary source for what
  the government did; not proof the policy works.
- **notice**: a Federal Register notice or a government data file. The primary document itself.
- **analysis**: KFF, RAND, Commonwealth Fund, Urban, Justice in Aging and similar. Independent
  analysis with its own numbers; cite them as that group's analysis.

## What each is good for

- **pitch**: a policy change, a dollar figure, a deadline or a disparity that changes something for
  readers, with a primary document a reporter can stand on. Medicare, Medicaid, caregiving, nursing
  homes, drug prices, insurance, consumer finance and scams travel best. Open enrollment, effective
  dates and comment deadlines are clocks.
  - A press release pitches only when you can name `primary_test`: the filing, trial registry
    entry, agency dataset or study that would show whether the claim holds. The story is the test,
    not the announcement.
  - **Standouts.** If a release is genuinely remarkable or could be groundbreaking (a first, a
    large effect in a serious disease, a major policy reversal) and you can't name the test yet,
    keep it anyway: route it to pitch with `standout: true` and say why in `standout_reason`. The
    pitch agents will find the independent test before writing. Use this rarely; most releases are
    not standouts.
- **tfm**: a release making a claim people will repeat ("75% tumor response", "cuts risk by 62%")
  that can be checked against what the number means and what the registry or data shows. The
  release is the claim; the receipt is the primary source. Best fit of all four for "Is it real?".
- **roundup**: a number a reader can use from an agency or an independent analysis (premiums up 9%,
  a new scam warning, a coverage deadline). Never a company's claim as a roundup fact.
- **brief**: rarely. Only where a site in the list covers it (food assistance and SNAP for The Kind
  Grid, recalls for Recall Register, menopause care for the menopause site). SeniorLiving.com is
  never a destination.

## How to judge

- "A number changed" is not a story. It needs a reader consequence or a pattern across items.
- Several items about one policy are one idea with all their keys (two KFF analyses and a Justice
  in Aging FAQ on Medicaid work requirements are one story).
- Drop corporate news with no reader stake: partnerships, executive hires, funding rounds, market
  research reports for sale, adviser marketing ("what Medicare covers, from a financial planner").
- Drug and device trial releases: say the phase, the endpoint and whether there are survival or
  hard-outcome data. A response rate is not a survival benefit.
- A bar, not a quota. Most items are drops.
